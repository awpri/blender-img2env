# Verification — how each milestone is checked

Milestones come from `HANDOFF.md`. For each one: what the automated suite
already proves, and what still needs a photograph, a GPU or a human eye.

M1, M2 and M6 have now been run against the real photographs in `testphotos/`
(`IMG_7096.HEIC` and `IMG_7263.DNG`). M3–M5 and M7 still need Depth Pro weights
and a render, which is what the manual column is for.

```bash
make test          # 497 tests, no Blender and no torch, ~9 s
make smoke         # 17 checks inside a real Blender
```

`server/tests/test_real_photos.py` is the module that touches real pixels. It
skips itself when `testphotos/` is absent, so CI stays hermetic — the photos
are gitignored, being large and personal.

## M1 — EXIF solve, no ML

**Automated.** `server/tests/test_exif.py::test_m1_acceptance` runs the
handoff's acceptance criterion against a fixture built from the EXIF quoted in
`LIGHTING_ADDENDUM.md`:

| quantity | acceptance | measured |
|---|---|---|
| pitch | 3.3° ± 0.5 | 3.279° |
| roll | 0.7° ± 0.5 | 0.715° |
| heading | 76.9° | 76.944° |
| focal | 14 mm | 14 mm |

The four-orientation calibration set the handoff asked for is
`orientation_{1,3,6,8}_exif.json`: one physical camera pose, four ways of
holding the phone. `test_all_four_orientations_agree` requires them to solve to
the same pitch and roll within 0.25°. The vectors are hand-derived from the
physical pose rather than generated by the code, so a wrong axis map fails
instead of agreeing with itself — see `fixtures/README.md`.

**Confirmed against the real files.** The fixtures are now genuine
`exiftool -j -n -G0:1` dumps, and `test_real_photos.py` re-runs the acceptance
criterion through a live exiftool on `IMG_7096.HEIC`. Both photos solve with
**zero warnings**, which is the bar for an unmodified original.

Two things the real files corrected:

- **`IMG_7096` is HEIC, not ProRAW,** and it *kept its GPS coordinates*
  (46.5005 N, 7.7141 E, 1659 m). `LIGHTING_ADDENDUM.md` was written against a
  JPEG re-export that had lost them. So the solar half of the pipeline works on
  it after all, and the "stripped coordinates" warning is now tested by
  stripping the real fixture rather than by keeping a hand-made one.
- **An Apple ProRAW DNG carries the same tag name at three resolutions** —
  IFD0 (a reduced-resolution preview), SubIFD (the 8064 px Linear Raw) and
  SubIFD1 (a 2016 px *semantic mask*). A prefix match on `EXIF:SubIFD` hits
  both SubIFDs; picking the mask would have scaled the whole solve by a third
  and looked like bad depth. The lookup is now group-boundary aware.

**Still to do by hand.**

1. Shoot the four-orientation set for real. Same scene, same spot, portrait /
   portrait-upside-down / both landscapes. The current fixtures use
   hand-derived vectors on the real tag layout; real captures would close the
   loop. They should still agree within 0.25°.
2. Shoot the level-floor calibration frame. Phone vertical, level ground.
   `IMG_7263.DNG` is nearly this already — it solves to pitch −0.21°, roll
   +0.12° — but it was not shot deliberately level, so it confirms nothing on
   its own.

## M2 — camera into Blender

**Automated.** `test_coords.py` is 200+ cases on the rotation math with
synthetic gravity, written before any of it touched Blender. The two defining
properties are checked across a grid of pitch/roll/heading:

- `R @ gravity == (0, 0, -1)` — whatever the camera was doing, gravity ends up
  pointing at the floor.
- `azimuth(R @ forward) == heading` — the lens faces where the compass said.

Plus: orthonormality and det = +1 everywhere, the near-nadir fallback, the
Blender `-Z` forward convention, and `test_camera_and_sun_agree_on_where_north_is`,
which is the one that would catch the camera and the sun each being internally
correct but rotated relative to each other.

`make smoke` then builds the camera in Blender from a synthetic solve and
checks it faces 76.94° at +3.28° pitch with its up vector out of the ground.

**Still to do by hand.** The eye check the handoff specifies: solve a real
photo, look through the camera, and confirm the Blender floor grid sits on the
real ground and the horizon lands where the real one does. If the grid is
tipped 90°, the orientation branch is wrong — but M1's four-orientation test
should have caught that first.

If the backplate lines up in the middle and drifts at the edges, that is the
focal convention, not the rotation. See `DECISIONS.md` §2.

## M3 — depth → proxy mesh

**Automated.** `test_depth.py` renders analytic depth maps of a flat floor at a
known camera height and known pitch, then requires the solve to recover that
height within 2% — across heights 1.2–12 m and pitches −20° to +3.3°. Also:
roll-invariance (the plane's normal comes from gravity, so banking the camera
must not move the floor), robustness to objects standing on the ground, and
rejection of sky-only frames.

Discontinuity culling is checked to be scale-free: the same relative depth step
is culled at 2 m and at 200 m, which is why one threshold works for a rock in
the foreground and a ridge on the horizon.

Face winding is checked explicitly, because a reversed proxy is invisible in a
wireframe viewport and only shows up as a surface that catches no shadows.

`make smoke` builds the mesh in Blender and confirms it is a passive rigid body
with normals facing the camera.

### Depth Pro's metric scale is wrong on these photographs

Run against both reference files, using the people in them as the known object
the acceptance criterion calls for:

| photo | object | true distance | Depth Pro says | implied height |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `IMG_7263.DNG` | person, 495 px | 11.1 m | 2.20 m | **0.35 m** |
| `IMG_7096.HEIC` | hiker, 395 px | 13.5 m | 2.69 m | **0.34 m** |

An adult is 1.7 m. Both scenes under-read by **about 5x** at working distance,
and the far field is far worse — 4 km peaks report under 10 m, sky reports 8 m.

This was checked properly before being blamed on the model. The pixel
measurements were verified at 1:1; `f_px` was confirmed correct by the model's
own independent focal estimate (597 px passed, 635 px estimated); and four
configurations — resized and full resolution, supplied and estimated focal —
all agree within 6%. No focal convention can reconcile a 5x error: it would
need a 162-degree field of view.

The error is **progressive, not affine**: roughly 1.5x at 1 m, 5x at 12 m, 400x
at 4 km. So no single number fixes the whole frame. But one multiplier
calibrated at the distance you actually work at does recover the near and mid
field, which is where CG objects sit, collide and cast shadows.

```bash
python tools/calibrate_scale.py testphotos/IMG_7263.DNG \
    --top 4660 --bottom 5155 --x 3264 --height 1.75
# -> depth_scale = 5.03
```

Put that in the panel's **Depth scale** field. It is a property of the lens and
the kind of scene rather than of one photograph, so calibrate once and reuse.
With it, `IMG_7263` moves from a 0.55 m camera height to 2.59 m — plausible for
someone standing on those boulders, where 0.55 m never was.

The panel shows the field in red until it is set, and the solve returns a
warning, because an uncalibrated solve drops cubes at a fifth of the right size
and nothing else in the pipeline will tell you.

**Known follow-up:** the ground-plane inlier band is a fixed 0.15 m window, so
scaling depth by 5 also scales the noise and confidence drops (0.48 → 0.18 on
`IMG_7263`). The band should probably scale with the depth range. Left alone
for now because tuning it needs a scene with genuinely flat, known ground
rather than a boulder field.

**Still to do by hand — this is the acceptance test.** With `depth_scale` set,
add the drop-test cube (panel ▸ Proxy Geometry ▸ *Add Drop Test Cube*) and play
the timeline. **Resting height must be within 15% of ground truth.**

If it still lands wrong, check in this order: (1) the depth scale is actually
being sent — the solve response echoes `depth_scale`; (2) the focal convention;
(3) the ground-plane confidence in the panel.

## M4 — shadow proxy shader + compositor

**Automated (`make smoke`).** The material is asserted node by node: Mix Shader
Fac driven by `Is Camera Ray`, Transparent on input 2 (the camera-ray branch),
Principled on input 1, and the plate's Vector driven by **Window** coordinates —
the last of these being the thing that makes reflections pick up the real
ground. The compositor is asserted plate-under-CG, on both the 4.x and 5.x
node APIs.

**Still to do by hand.** The chrome sphere. Drop a sphere with metallic 1.0 and
roughness 0.0 onto the terrain and render. **It must reflect the actual ground
beneath it** — real colour, real texture — not a grey approximation. If it
reflects grey, the Window coordinate link is the first thing to check.

Cycles only. EEVEE Next evaluates `Is Camera Ray` differently and will not do
screen-space reflections off geometry it is not rendering.

## M5 — bounce proxy *(the highest-value milestone)*

**Automated (`make smoke`).** The ray-visibility split is asserted in both
directions, which is what stops light being counted twice:

| | shadow proxy | bounce proxy |
|---|---|---|
| camera | matte (shadow catcher) | invisible |
| diffuse / glossy | **off** | **on**, emissive |
| shadow | on | off |
| collision | yes | no |

`test_imaging.py` covers the strength calibration: feed it a plate whose median
already carries the right radiance for `irradiance × albedo / π` and the
multiplier must come back as exactly 1.

**Still to do by hand — the handoff's check.** Put a white diffuse sphere in
the scene. Render with the bounce proxy enabled, then hit *Toggle Bounce* and
render again. The toggle is symmetric on purpose: turning the bounce off also
hands indirect duties back to the shadow proxy, so the two renders are
genuinely comparable rather than one simply being darker.

What you are looking for: the lit render should show colour picked up from the
ground (green over grass, warm over stone) and should **not** be roughly twice
as bright — that would mean both proxies are emitting.

Worth doing indoors too. Interiors are the easier case: no sun, no sky worth
modelling, and every wall and lamp is already in the photograph.

## M6 — DNG lighting plate

**Automated.** Format routing, the two-plate contract, orientation (both plates
must come out in the same display space as the intrinsics, or the
Window-projected reprojection lands half a frame out), and the EXR write
carrying values above 1.0 — which is the entire point, and would otherwise be
silently clamped by a backend without OpenEXR support.

Opcode detection is tested including the negative case: `FixVignetteRadial` is
a shading opcode and must not be reported as a geometry warning.

**Confirmed against `IMG_7263.DNG`, and the route there changed.**

LibRaw 0.22 cannot open the file at all — DNG 1.7 with JPEG XL compression
returns "Unsupported file format or not RAW file" — so the blueprint's
`rawpy → EXR` path fails at the first call on this camera. macOS Core Image
reads it natively, and with `boostAmount = 0` returns linear data with the
headroom intact:

| | |
|---|---|
| max | 2.890 |
| median | 0.210 |
| above 1.0 | 0.52% of pixels |
| EXR on disk | 36 MB at 2048 px long edge |

Both plates come out display-oriented at 6048×8064, matching the intrinsics.
`test_real_photos.py` pins all of this, including the LibRaw failure — so a
future rawpy release that quietly fixes it gets noticed rather than assumed.

The distortion question is settled and was never real on this camera: the DNG
is `Linear Raw` with no opcodes at all. `DECISIONS.md` §1 has the evidence.

**Still to do by hand.** Compare a render lit by the linear EXR bounce proxy
against one lit by the display PNG. The EXR is now wired into the bounce proxy
automatically when the solve produced one, and the panel says which it used.
The difference should be most obvious in the sky's contribution — that is where
the clipping was.

## M7 — automatic shadow extraction + gobo

**Automated.** The fallback contract: when `compphoto/Intrinsic` is missing the
mask still comes back, the method is reported, and the caveat travels with it.
Masks are bounded so the worst case is a slightly-too-dark patch rather than a
hole punched in the sunlight.

The flagged gotcha is covered directly in `make smoke`: a bake that raises
mid-render must leave the scene exactly as it found it — materials, samples,
resolution, compositor, camera, view transform. The test simulates a render
failure inside the context manager and diffs a snapshot.

`test_imaging.py` pins the load-bearing limitation: **the ratio only sees
shadows smaller than its own blur kernel.** Set `mask_blur` too small and the
mask comes back nearly blank, which looks like the extraction failing rather
than a knob in the wrong place.

**Still to do by hand.** Install `compphoto/Intrinsic`, run *Extract Shade
Mask*, and compare its output against the retinex fallback on a shot with
mixed terrain — dark rock on grass is the case where they should diverge, and
where intrinsic decomposition should win. Then bake the gobo and confirm a CG
object set down in dappled shade receives broken light and casts a shadow that
breaks up in the same pattern.

## Calibration, once, then never again

From `BLUEPRINT.md`, still true:

1. **Gravity axes** — the level-floor shot. Covered by M1 above.
2. **Sky rotation** — shoot something with a hard shadow, solve, render a test
   cube, compare shadow directions. Any constant error goes into
   `sky_rotation_offset` and stays there. Nishita's zero-rotation reference is
   not true north and the sign runs opposite to a compass bearing, so expect a
   non-zero value here; that is the knob's whole purpose.
3. **Height** — solve a photo where you know your eye height and compare
   against the reported camera height. A consistent bias points at the
   orientation branch.
